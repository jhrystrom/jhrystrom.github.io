Method & sources

Required now = goal ÷ Π(1 − rᵢ) over each year between your current and target age, where rᵢ is the annual decline rate for that age and sex. Strength rates are annual steps by decade. Values in between are interpolations, so read the result as an estimate, not a prescription.

VO₂max decline: per-decade rates are set at decade midpoints (25, 35 … 75), interpolated linearly in between, and converted to annual rates by r = 1 − (1 − d)1/10. Men: 3, 6, 10, 14, 18, 25%/decade. Women: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18%/decade. The ends come from the two large longitudinal cohorts with directly measured VO₂ (BLSA [1], HUNT [2]), as pooled by Letnes et al. [3]: about 3%/decade in the 20s, rising to 15–20% in women and 20–25% in men after 70, with men declining faster from the 40s onward. The mid-life values are interpolated. As a check, starting from typical young-adult values (45 ml/kg/min for men, 36 for women), from age 25 to 65 the curves lose about 0.39 ml/kg/min per year for men, against 0.40 in the cross-sectional meta-analysis [4]. For women they lose about 0.28, against 0.35–0.44 [5], so the women's curve may be slightly optimistic in mid-life. The meta-analyses report roughly linear absolute decline, which means the percentage loss grows with age. We use longitudinal rather than cross-sectional data because cross-sectional studies underestimate decline in old age through survivor bias [3].

Does being fit slow the decline? Mostly not in percentage terms. Relative decline was similar across sedentary, active and endurance-trained groups in both meta-analyses [4, 5], and across activity quartiles in BLSA [1]. HUNT found that people who kept up vigorous activity held on to more fitness [2]. Fitter people stay ahead mainly because they start higher, which is the point of training now. The decline-scenario setting stands in for that variation between people.

Cardio target: activity cost = MET₆₀₊ × 2.7 ml/kg/min, using the Older Adult Compendium [6], which measured energy costs in people aged 60 and over against their lower resting metabolic rate. Required VO₂max = cost ÷ sustainable fraction, because an activity you want to keep up for more than a few minutes should sit well below your max. The "stay independent" preset uses the VO₂max levels below which loss of independence becomes likely: 18 ml/kg/min for men and 15 for women [7].

  1. Fleg JL et al. Accelerated longitudinal decline of aerobic capacity in healthy older adults. Circulation 2005;112:674–682. (Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, n = 810, ages 21–87. Decline accelerates from 3–6%/decade in the 20s–30s to >20%/decade in the 70s, is larger in men from the 40s, and is similar across activity levels.)
  2. Letnes JM et al. Age-related change in peak oxygen uptake and change of cardiovascular risk factors. The HUNT Study. Prog Cardiovasc Dis 2020;63:730–737. (n = 1,431, directly measured VO₂peak 10 years apart. Decline goes from 3% in the 20s to about 20% in the 70s and is more pronounced in men.)
  3. Letnes JM, Nes BM, Wisløff U. Age-related decline in peak oxygen uptake: cross-sectional vs. longitudinal findings. A review. Int J Cardiol Cardiovasc Risk Prev 2023;16:200171.
  4. Wilson TM, Tanaka H. Meta-analysis of the age-associated decline in maximal aerobic capacity in men: relation to training status. Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 2000;278:H829–H834. (242 studies, 13,828 men. −4.0 to −4.6 ml/kg/min per decade, no difference by training status.)
  5. Fitzgerald MD et al. Age-related declines in maximal aerobic capacity in regularly exercising vs. sedentary women: a meta-analysis. J Appl Physiol 1997;83:160–165. (109 studies, 4,884 women. −3.5 to −6.2 ml/kg/min per decade, about −10%/decade relative to age 25 in every group.)
  6. Willis EA et al. Older Adult Compendium of Physical Activities: energy costs of human activities in adults aged 60 and older. J Sport Health Sci 2024;13:13–17. (MET₆₀₊ values for the presets.)
  7. Shephard RJ. Maximal oxygen intake and independence in old age. Br J Sports Med 2009;43:342–346.
  8. Goodpaster BH et al. The loss of skeletal muscle strength, mass, and quality in older adults: the Health ABC Study. J Gerontol A 2006;61:1059–1064. (Knee-extensor strength loss in the 70s: ~3.4%/yr in men, ~2.6%/yr in women.)
  9. Lindle RS et al. Age and gender comparisons of muscle strength in 654 women and men aged 20–93 yr. J Appl Physiol 1997;83:1581–1587. (Strength loss begins in midlife and accelerates with age. Used for the pre-70 shape.)
  10. Mandsager K et al. Association of cardiorespiratory fitness with long-term mortality. JAMA Netw Open 2018;1:e183605. (Cited in Outlive for the importance of VO₂max.)
