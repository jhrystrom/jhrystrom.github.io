Many people have a tendency to equate Artificial Intelligence (AI) with Machine Learning (ML). Some even extend this, claiming that only deep learning is *true* AI. I sympathize with this notion. After all, there is something magical about the way we can achieve amazing results by throwing enough data and compute at a problem. Solving a problem the old-fashioned way with hard-coded rules is not nearly so mysterious.

Nowhere is this sentiment more true than in AI competitions. Competitors often battle to create [the most sophisticated ensembles of awesome ML models](https://www.kaggle.com/c/tabular-playground-series-nov-2021/code). The winner is whoever can squeeze the most performance out of the test set.

I must admit it was the same attitude my team, Superficial Intelligence, and I had when we entered the [Danish AI championships](https://dmiai.dk/en/). There were four tasks to conquer: [Where’s Waldo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where%27s_Wally%3F); [predicting the ratings of movie reviews](https://huggingface.co/datasets/rotten_tomatoes); driving a simulated car on a highway; and creating an IQ-test solver. After a week of sweat, tears, late and hard work we submitted our solutions. The results came in: we finished second place!

However, our rise to the podium is not a story of building complex ensembles of magnificent models. Quite the opposite. In only one of the four tasks, the movie ratings, did we use *any* ML to solve the problem. The rests used a combination of heuristics and tricks to solve the tasks. Why this worked (and where it didn’t) taught me important lessons about working with ML. Lessons I hope to share with you.

Let’s start with the task where we actually used ML: predicting movie ratings. This task is a classic in [Natural Language Processing (NLP)](https://www.ibm.com/cloud/learn/natural-language-processing). NLP is notoriously difficult to solve with rules. This is embodied in the famous quip by the researcher Frederick Jelinek: [“Every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recognizer goes up”](https://quotepark.com/quotes/1777032-fred-jelinek-every-time-i-fire-a-linguist-the-performance-of-o/). Luckily, many problems can be solved by [fine-tuning enormous language models with large amounts of data](https://huggingface.co/blog/large-language-models). Predicting movie ratings is one such problem: There are many models already trained for similar tasks (shout out to [the incredible team at HuggingFace!](https://huggingface.co/)), and the training data is [fairly easy to access](https://github.com/Team-Superficial-Intelligence/dmiai-comp/blob/uc2/movie-reviews/scraper.py). Therefore, it makes sense that we would succeed with ML and my best guess is that most teams followed a similar approach.

The other tasks did not share this characteristic. The IQ task is an obvious example. [Reasoning is notoriously difficult](https://towardsdatascience.com/a-critical-appraisal-of-deep-learning-1b154695dddf) for even the most advanced deep learning systems. Furthermore, we were only provided with a single example, making training a classifier all but impossible. We therefore ended up coding up a bunch of rules, like “check if the pattern is a clockwise rotation” or “is the figures following [Game of Life](https://playgameoflife.com/) logic?” In the end, no team were particularly successful on this task (ours included).

The last two tasks lie somewhere in between having obvious ML solutions and easier hard-coded ones. Take the Where’s Waldo task. Here we were given mosaics of different Where’s Waldo maps and had to find him. The ML approach would be to scrape all the waldo maps you could find (or find [someone who has](https://www.kaggle.com/residentmario/wheres-waldo)) and then create a [convolutional neural network](https://towardsdatascience.com/a-comprehensive-guide-to-convolutional-neural-networks-the-eli5-way-3bd2b1164a53) to classify the images, possibly with a bunch of data augmentation thrown in to increase the amount of training data. This is exactly the approach we initially pursued. However, after days of hard work, we realized that the model, although performing great on the training data, had trouble [generalizing to the validation set](https://deeplizard.com/learn/video/DEMmkFC6IGM).

In this hour of darkness, an insight struck: There is only a limited number of Where’s Waldo maps on the internet. For all (or at least most) of these we had meticulously created bounding boxes to train our models. But what if instead of training a model, we just did a direct template search? From manually going through the given examples, we could see that there wasn’t any significant data augmentation apart from the random cropping. We quickly implemented the [template matching algorithm](https://www.pyimagesearch.com/2021/03/22/opencv-template-matching-cv2-matchtemplate/) and let it run on the validation set. We were astonished to find that this simple method outperformed our neural network — and even most of the other teams! On the final evaluation set, this approach gave us a decent score — one that I’m sure we could have improved by implementing some scaling. Unfortunately, we didn’t have time for that given the time we’d spent on the deep learning.

The story is similar for the driving simulation. The standard ML approach would be to deploy some sort of [reinforcement learning](https://www.techtarget.com/searchenterpriseai/definition/reinforcement-learning#:~:text=Reinforcement%20learning%20is%20a%20machine,learn%20through%20trial%20and%20error.) — a technique that is incredibly successful at solving a wide variety of games from [Atari](https://deepmind.com/research/publications/2019/playing-atari-deep-reinforcement-learning) to [Go](https://deepmind.com/research/case-studies/alphago-the-story-so-far). For a long time, we also struggled with this approach but kept hitting a wall (and other cars!). It proved extremely difficult to get training loop to integrate with the game API.

In the end we scrapped the reinforcement learning for a simple rule: What if we made the car accelerate until it reached the car in front and then just kept that distance? This is drivers ed 101, and probably the simplest rule you could think of. However, it proved vastly better score than the reinforcement learning approach. We ended up adding a few extra rules to allow over car to navigate better and go faster and farther, but all in all it was no more than 120 lines of code.

So what I learn from this experience? Apart from the irony of the name “Team Superficial Intelligence”, my main takeaway is brilliantly captured by Eugene Yang: [“The first rule of machine learning: start without machine learning”](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/first-rule-of-ml/). If a problem can be tackled without the need for machine learning, it is probably a better solution. At least, it is worth a try to spend some time brainstorming potential non-ML solutions. Worst case: You waste a bit of time. Best case: You get a (literal or metaphorical) podium position.
