The ubiquitous power of Big Tech is undeniable. [Facebook influence the outcomes of elections](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2019/oct/21/facebook-us-2020-elections-foreign-interference-russia) and creates single-minded echo chambers, [Baidu and Alibaba help the Chinese government to build elaborate profiles](https://foreignpolicy.com/2020/12/23/china-tech-giants-process-stolen-data-spy-agencies/) and control all facets of its citizens lives, tech companies are increasingly shaping our future. The extend of this seemingly all-encompassing force can seem impossible to grasp. Nevertheless, understanding the power of Big Tech is essential for reclaiming control of the future.

Luckily, we have frameworks to help us understand force and *Surveillance Capitalism* is such a framework. Surveillance Capitalism, introduced by Harvard professor [Shoshanna Zuboff](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2019/oct/04/shoshana-zuboff-surveillance-capitalism-assault-human-automomy-digital-privacy) in her book [“the Age of Surveillance Capitalism”](https://www.publicaffairsbooks.com/titles/shoshana-zuboff/the-age-of-surveillance-capitalism/9781610395694/), describes the history and underlying imperatives of Big Tech. Facilitated by the shocks of the dotcom crash and 9/11, surveillance capitalism promised enormous profits by following its two imperatives:

- the \[extraction imperative (every aspect of users’ lives should be collected) and
- the prediction imperative (every action should be predicted, monetized, and ultimately controlled)\](https://www.diggitmagazine.com/wiki/surveillance-capitalism).\
  Combined, the imperatives paved the path for the total dominance of tech companies by making them control ever more of their users’ lives — which soon meant everyone’s lives.

The fundamental force powering these imperatives is what Zuboff terms *machine intelligence*— basically, a catch-all term for big data, machine learning and the like. Reading this got me feeling like the German soldiers in the [legendary Mitchell and Webb sketch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8JOpPNra4bw&ab_channel=ThatLook) as depicted by the GIF below.

My reaction was based on the scope of the problem. Surveillance Capitalism threatens the very thing that makes us human: our autonomy. By making our every move into prediction products, we are subliminally herded into predictable, profit-maximizing paths. Meanwhile we — the data scientists — are paving this path with ever more techniques to extract, predict, and understand data. Coupled with the fact that [the competitiveness of Surveillance Capitalism is potentially detrimental to AI safety](https://www.cold-takes.com/making-the-best-of-the-most-important-century/#the-competition-frame), I felt the despair sneaking in.

However, the more I thought about the implications, the more I saw reasons for hope. One such reason is that while machine intelligence underpins surveillance capitalism that development is not inevitable. There are many broadly beneficial uses of machine learning from [detecting cancer](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-020-03157-9#:~:text=Bagci%20and%20his%20team%20have,rate%20that%20radiologists%20typically%20achieve.) to [monitoring the health of the rain forest](https://techmonitor.ai/leadership/innovation/sounds-forest-bio-acoustic-analysis-helping-fight-illegal-logging) that don’t compromise the autonomy of individuals. These solutions help make the world a better place by empowering people instead of marginalizing them. We should therefore be careful to treat not the symptoms but the underlying disease.

The efforts to counteract surveillance capitalism can, unfortunately, backfire. An example of this is the idea of [personal data ownership](https://www.economist.com/open-future/2019/01/21/we-need-to-own-our-data-as-a-human-right-and-be-compensated-for-it). This ideally empowers people by allowing them to control to whom they sell their data. At least that is the intention. Unfortunately, this idea inadvertently legitimizes one of the core foundations of surveillance capitalism, namely, that human experience is for sale. This, arguably much more fundamental proposition, is thus swept under the carpet instead of being openly discussed. The result may be that personal data ownership actually strengthens surveillance capitalism instead of combatting it.

Another example is the outrage over published internal research like the paper by [Kramer et al.](https://www.pnas.org/content/111/24/8788) from Facebook on massive emotional contagion. In the paper, the researchers found that changing the newsfeeds of users changed the sentiment of their expressed emotions significantly (although the effect size was tiny). The sparked outrage as multiple news sites and journal articles critiqued the lack of informed consent as the subjects were subliminally manipulated. While this is an important ethical point, the methods deployed in the study are no harsher than the [business-as-usual procedures operating at Facebook](https://www.nytimes.com/live/2021/10/05/technology/facebook-whistleblower-frances-haugen). The study therefore gives us a glimpse of how Facebook approaches experimentation, which can be valuable for mounting a broad critique. However, if we focus to much on a specific study, it might simply make Facebook (and other companies) clamp up about their operations, leaving the rest of us in the dark.

So, what can data scientists actually do? One first step could be to adopt a universal [Hippocratic Oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Oath) of data science. Just like the medical profession has power for both great harm and benefit, so too have the data scientists. And likewise, we need an oath to keep our behavior beneficial. The oath should ideally include respect for privacy and autonomy as core principles, as well as promising an active effort to combat bias in data and modeling. There are already some attempts at this, notably [this effort from the National Academy of Sciences](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532770/). However, to be successful the oath needs to be widely adopted in the data science community — for both practitioners and companies. This way the community can legitimize whistleblowers, who currently risk their careers delivering important messages. With a Hippocratic Oath of data science, potential whistleblowers can rest assured that the community has their back.

Another essential role for data scientists in dismantling the cruel logic of surveillance capitalism is to propose viable alternatives. We need a serious discussion about how machine learning and data can be used to improve the world. A difficult aspect of this question is to find commercial alternatives to the enormous profits of surveillance capitalism. It would be almost impossible to convince Google and the other tech giants to change their ways (or dethrone them ethically) without having a commercially viable alternative. I, unfortunately, have no answer to this question (yet) but I hope we, as a community, can come up with some.

Finally, data practitioners need to collaborate with regulators. The blazing pace of AI development makes it an insurmountable task for regulators to keep up. The result is often stillborn legislation of the technologies of yesteryear that [keep lacking behind](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-06-25/eu-s-broken-gdpr-needs-fixing-departing-privacy-chief-warns) the evolving paradigms of technology and business. Data practitioners could help regulators bridge the gap. Armed with practical knowledge of the current state-of-the-art and visions of a future with beneficial use of AI, we can guide the path to that future.

Solving the problems posed by surveillance capitalism will be immensely difficult. It will provide a challenge much different from how we usually approach problems. Nevertheless, the stakes are high: Do we want a future with or without human autonomy? If we succeed, we can help change course towards a future where we are empowered by technology instead of enslaved by imperatives. And that is a cause worth fighting for.
