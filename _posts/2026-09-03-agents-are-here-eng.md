This summer there have been several examples of AI agents from among others OpenAI "breaking out" and attempting on their own [to hack other companies' systems](https://huggingface.co/blog/agent-intrusion-technical-timeline). But it is not only a risk in the US. Here at home too we are talking about the future bringing "AI agent" systems and what risks that can mean.

Haven’t you tried using ChatGPT to write an email, get good advice, translate a text, or maybe even "vibe code" a website?

But did you know that ChatGPT is no longer "just" a language model? Depending on how much you pay, the same "prompt" can set off systems as different as the original ChatGPT and former search engines. The big difference is that AI systems have become *agentic*.

Agent systems are already here today. The vast majority of us use agent systems at work without knowing it. And if we want to ensure that it is us who have control over the systems — and not the other way around — then we need to act now.

## From "chat" to "agent"
When ChatGPT came out in 2022, it gave the most likely answer to whatever the user had "prompted". People quickly discovered that you could get well-formulated answers to any question in any style. Many also discovered that you could not take the answers at face value; "chat" [hallucinated](https://www.npr.org/2022/12/19/1143912956/chatgpt-ai-chatbot-homework-academia), was [bad at math](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/14/7/698), and generally had a tendency to [bullshit](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/01/06/podcasts/transcript-ezra-klein-interviews-gary-marcus.html).

Since then technological development has exploded. AI platforms such as ChatGPT and Claude are today "agentic". [They can do four things](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.21848) that ChatGPT struggled with: 1) They can *plan* how they will approach a task instead of answering right away. 2) They can use *tools* such as calculators or other computer programs. 3) They can solve *complex problems* such as mathematical proofs. And 4) They have become *autonomous* and can work independently for longer periods of time.

## A "simple" question – an agentic answer
All of that happens without the user necessarily being aware of it. The agentic shift is fundamentally about interactions with AI systems having become agentic "by default". Recently I asked the newest paid version of ChatGPT how many Danish PhD students study abroad. The simple question took the AI system 43 minutes to answer. During those 43 minutes the system visited a countless number of websites, wrote a lot of code, cross-referenced numbers, and produced graphs. None of this had I explicitly asked for; I just wanted a solid estimate.

I also asked the free version of ChatGPT. It answered in 5 seconds after having visited 81 websites. Even this answer is relatively agentic; the system first made a plan and then actively searched for information on the open internet. Had I asked the old ChatGPT the same question, it would probably have hallucinated an answer.

## "With great powers" come great risks
AI agents are not something that "is coming"; agents are already here. The same properties – planning, tool use, goal complexity, autonomy – that made ChatGPT count PhD students in 43 minutes enabled this summer’s sophisticated hacking attacks. How agentic systems are depends on how much you are willing to pay and [how risk-willing you are, voluntarily or involuntarily](https://politiken.dk/danmark/politik/folkemoedet/art10813910/Han-kan-godt-forestille-sig-et-folkem%C3%B8de-kun-med-ai-agenter).

Even my PhD counting attempt could have gone completely wrong. When the agent could not find statistics from Harvard, a system with more autonomy or more sophisticated tool use could have tried to break into Harvard’s database. The thought experiment is strikingly similar to this summer’s hacking fiascos. Hacking is illegal. But since you cannot imprison computer code [yet], it would have been either me or OpenAI’s management who should have gone to jail. [The law is not yet clarified.](https://www.economist.com/science-and-technology/2026/08/06/should-ai-labs-be-treated-like-the-owners-of-dangerous-animals?trk=feed-detail_comments-list_comment-text)

The government has announced that AI agents should be used to [make the public administration more efficient](https://www.berlingske.dk/politik/regeringen-vil-frigoere-30000-aarsvaerk-i-det-offentlige-med-kunstig-intelligens?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=AXbw80RUH-PiE587mFX0Twfr_p8qNisF1Q3qwpkj_oW7Ik6m342IJByI-v71MMTUWgE%3D&gaa_ts=6a859f03&gaa_sig=Op4ROUBSCbSQc4QU58P-bY2D2F4UwlUcDZ8jRS_nFGW-qJ1AGFzNYgxE5g54QAT5-YWU0kGcRXusi4KR4N_vlg%3D%3D). A good goal, but it requires a more fundamental debate about how we steer the agents. At Oxford University my colleagues and I have [examined over 1,300 scientific articles that evaluate AI agents](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/IASEAI/article/view/43059/50619). The result was depressing: None of the evaluations check whether AI agents meet the fundamental requirements that apply to public administration.

If there is one thing my own research has shown me, it is this: more agentic systems *are* wilder. They provide wilder possibilities, but also entail wilder risks. No one sums it up better than Spider-Man’s Uncle Ben: "With great powers comes great responsibility". Therefore it is now that we need a debate about how to set the framework for agent systems. Otherwise we all risk, with simple prompts, setting agents in motion that can do real harm.
